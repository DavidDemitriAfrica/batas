Batas · Philippine laws, 1946 to 2026

What does the Philippine Congress actually pass?

Every Republic Act since 1946, 12,322 of them, sorted by what it does. Six in ten are about a single named place: one school, one hospital, one road, one barangay, one town's holiday.

Scroll

01

Each dot is one Republic Act, stacked in the year it was approved, from July 1946 to September 2026.

There are none from 1973 to 1986. Marcos closed Congress when he declared martial law in 1972, and the next Congress did not sit until 1987. The busiest year was 1969, with 668 laws.

02

Here the laws are grouped by what they do. Only 3,032, about one in four, are national: a budget, a tax, a code, a new agency, a right that applies everywhere.

03

1,867 are for one company or one person: a franchise for a radio station or a power plant, citizenship for a basketball player.

04

The other 7,423, six in ten, are about one named place: a high school in a barangay, more beds for a district hospital, a national road, a new barangay, a holiday for a town's founding day.

That is by count. Since 2004, national laws have taken up 71% of the days the Senate spent debating bills that became law (Effort).

05

Most of these laws name a town, so we can put them on a map. 6,762 local laws are placed, 5,866 of them at a town named in the title.

Each cluster is a town, sized by the number of laws that name it. Manila and Quezon City lead, with 63 laws each.

06

The biggest group is schools. 3,050 laws create a high school, split an annex from its parent school, turn a school into a college, or rename one after someone.

07

The mix changes from one Congress to the next. The 8th Congress (1987 to 1992), the first under the 1987 Constitution, passed 999 laws, 79% of them local. The 14th (2007 to 2010) reached 84%.

08

Laws are also signed in batches. Here each law sits on the day it was approved, stacked with the others from that day.

Before martial law, Congress sent its bills to the President at the end of each session in May, and most were signed in June. On June 21, 1969, 506 laws were approved.

Waves

Kinds of local law, Congress by Congress

Some kinds of local law rise and fall together from one Congress to the next. The changes usually follow a change in who has the power to decide, or in what the national budget pays for.

After the Barrio Charter of 1959 let provincial boards create and rename barrios, Congress mostly stopped doing either. After a 2017 law made tuition free at state universities and colleges, laws for new campuses came back. Between the 13th and 14th Congresses, the number of laws splitting annexes from their parent high schools went from 2 to 151.

Each panel counts laws per Congress and has its own scale, printed at the top. The break separates the 1st to 7th Congresses (1946 to 1972) from the 8th to 20th (1987 on). The 20th Congress is still in session.

Bills

Most bills never become law

BetterGov's open record of the House of Representatives lists every House bill filed since 1987, 143,156 of them. We sorted their titles with the same rules we used for the laws.

Members of the 8th Congress, the first under the 1987 Constitution, filed 35,168 bills, and 87% asked for something in one place: a school building, a barangay hall, a deep well, a stretch of concrete road. That Congress passed 786 local laws in all, so at most 3 in 100 of these bills became law.

Local bill filings peaked in 1988, at 15,181, and had fallen by 1990, the year the national budget began giving each member a lump sum for projects in their district: the Countrywide Development Fund, later the PDAF. No year since has had more than 5,284.

Effort

Counting the Senate's time instead of laws

A count of laws gives a renamed school the same weight as the national budget. The Senate's record shows how long each bill spent on the floor, so we can weigh laws by that instead. It starts with the 13th Congress, so this section covers the 13th to the 19th (2004 to 2025): 2,980 laws, of which we traced 2,938 to the Senate record.

Local laws were 71% of the laws passed in those years. They took 59% of the Senate's floor days and 24% of its days of debate. National laws were 20% of the laws, 32% of the floor days and 71% of the days of debate.

The floor-day count favours local laws, because the Senate takes them up in groups and every law in a group gets the full day. 964 of the 2,095 local laws were sponsored in a single speech covering many bills, or approved with others in one motion. The median local law passed third reading on a day when the Senate passed 24 bills that became law; the median national law, on a day with 5. On January 31, 2022, the Senate approved 151 bills on third reading that later became law.

The President can certify a bill as urgent, which lets each chamber pass it on second and third reading on the same day. 121 laws in these years were certified, and 111 of them were national. Ten were local, all from 2007 to 2010. The certified laws were 4% of all laws, 8% of the floor days and 33% of the days of debate.

Certifications come from the Senate record and from lists published by the Presidential Legislative Liaison Office for the 18th and 19th Congresses. The two agree on 31 of the 35 laws on the office's lists; the other 4 were certified to the House only. The House record has no comparable floor history, so the House's time is not counted.

Promises

Hospital beds the laws promise

A law that raises a hospital's bed capacity does not pay for the beds; the money has to come later, in the budget. We checked each of these laws against PhilHealth's list of accredited hospitals, which gives the number of beds each hospital runs.

We matched 53 government hospitals whose bed capacity Congress raised by law since 2016. 47 of them are accredited today for fewer beds than their law says. Laws from 2023 on have had little time to take effect, but the older ones show the same gap: of the 44 hospitals whose law passed from 2016 to 2022, 38 are still below it.

A gap does not prove that nothing was built. Hospitals grow in stages, and PhilHealth accredits only beds that are licensed and in use. Still, the gaps are large, which suggests that many of these laws passed without the money to carry them out.

Most school laws name a real school, often one already open

Of the 1,557 laws from 1987 to 2023 that established, separated or converted a public school, we found the school the law names, in the right town, in DepEd's lists for 85%. We could not find 5%.

DepEd's lists begin in 2017, so for laws passed since 2019 we can also see whether the school existed before the law. Of the 107 we matched, 68% were in DepEd's list before the law was approved, including 22 of the 35 laws that "establish" a school. Many school laws give a new status or name to a school that is already running.

Authors

Who writes local laws

For bills filed since 2004, the House record names a principal author. We linked 2,095 local laws to the House bill they came from, and 1,746 of them to a district representative in OpenHalalan's record of winners, which gives the province they represent.

A common claim is that political families use laws like these to reward their base. We tested a simple version of it. A seat is a family seat when someone else with the representative's surname won the governorship, the vice governorship, another House seat, or a mayoralty in the same province in the same election, or held a House seat there in one of the two elections before. By this rule 50% of district terms were family seats.

Family seats did not produce more local laws. Representatives in family seats were the principal authors of 1.21 local laws per term. Other representatives averaged 1.30. The intervals overlap, and the gap is not consistent from one Congress to the next: family seats averaged more in two Congresses, about the same in two, and fewer in three.

Surnames are a rough marker of kinship. Unrelated people can share a common surname, and relatives can have different ones. OpenHalalan's records for 2004 to 2013 come from an older source that could not be checked against ballots. The figures are averages over many seats and say nothing about any one representative.

Calendar

When laws are signed

84% of laws before martial law were approved in June, after each regular session closed in May. Since 1987 the share is 10%, and laws are signed all year.

Data

How this was made

Sources

  • LawPhil Project (Arellano Law Foundation): the list and full text of every Republic Act. 309 acts missing from LawPhil come from the Chan Robles Virtual Law Library, and 22 that neither library has, including eight national budgets, from the House record.
  • BetterGov open-congress-data: every House bill since 1987, its author and its history, and the Senate's floor history of each bill since 2004 (CC0).
  • Presidential Legislative Liaison Office: bills certified for immediate enactment in the 18th and 19th Congresses, from the Internet Archive's copy of its site.
  • OpenHalalan (Leung, Alejandro, Acuna, Buot, Go and Nable 2026): winners of House, provincial and municipal races (ODbL).
  • DepEd machine-ready files: public schools and enrolment, school years 2017-18 to 2025-26.
  • PhilHealth: accredited hospitals and infirmaries, July 31, 2026.
  • Philippine Internal Migration Dataset: the PSGC list of towns, former names and census populations (PSA).
  • faeldon/philippines-json-maps: PSA 2023 boundaries of provinces and towns.

How the laws were sorted

Titles of Philippine laws follow fixed formulas ("An Act Establishing a National High School in Barangay ..."), so an ordered list of text rules sorts them well. We checked the rules against 400 laws drawn at random and labelled by hand: they agreed on 91.5% of laws for the nineteen kinds and 96.8% for local, private or national. After fixing the rules that failed, a fresh sample of 200 gave 92.0% and 98.5%.

Places come from the titles. A town counts only when its name is unique in the country or it sits inside a province the title also names. The enacting Congress comes from the act's header, else the dates the chambers passed it, else the House record, else the approval date.

Limits

  • Titles describe laws, not their effects. A law can be local in title and national in effect, and the reverse.
  • Three act numbers are in no source we could find.
  • Vetoed bills, and bills that died in the Senate, appear only through the House record.
  • A school or hospital we did not find may exist under another name, or in a list we did not check.
  • Floor days and days of debate cover the Senate from 2004 on. The House record has no comparable history.
  • Authors and family seats cover the 13th to 19th Congresses and the district representatives we could match to OpenHalalan's list of winners. Party-list members are not included.

Files

  • laws.csv: one row per Republic Act, with its kind, places, sources and links.
  • school_laws.csv and hospital_laws.csv: each law with the school or hospital we matched.
  • law_effort.csv: Senate floor days, days of debate and urgent certification for each law since 2004.
  • law_authors.csv and rep_terms.csv: authors and representative terms.
  • CODEBOOK.md explains every column; every download is logged in data/provenance.jsonl.
