Batas · an atlas of Philippine law

What does the Philippine Congress actually pass?

Every Republic Act since 1946, 12,322 of them, sorted by what it does. Six in ten are about a single named place: one school, one hospital, one road, one barangay, one town's holiday.

Scroll

01

Each dot is one Republic Act, stacked in the year it was approved, from July 1946 to September 2026.

The gap runs from martial law in 1972 to the new Congress of 1987: there was no Congress in between, and no Republic Acts. The busiest year was 1969, with 668 laws.

02

Sort the dots by what each law does. Only 3,028, about one law in four, are national: a budget, a tax, a code, a new agency, a right that applies to everyone.

03

1,867 are for one company or one person: a franchise for a radio station or a power plant, citizenship for a basketball player.

04

The other 7,427, six in ten, are about one named place: a high school in a barangay, more beds for a district hospital, a national road, a new barangay, a town's founding day off.

05

Most of these laws name their town, so we can put each one where it belongs. 6,762 local laws, 5,866 of them placed at a town they name, draw the country.

Each cluster is a town: the bigger it is, the more laws name it. Manila and Quezon City lead, with 63 laws each.

06

The biggest kind is schools. 3,050 laws create a high school, split an annex from its parent school, turn a school into a college, or rename one after someone.

07

The mix changes from one Congress to the next. The first Congress under the 1987 Constitution, the 8th (1987 to 1992), passed 999 laws, 79% of them local. The 14th (2007 to 2010) went further: 84%.

08

And laws arrive in batches. Here every law sits on the day it was approved, stacked with the others from that day.

Before martial law, Congress sent its bills to the President at the end of each session in May, and most were signed in June. On June 21, 1969, 506 laws were approved in a single day.

Waves

Laws come in fashions

Inside the local share, particular kinds of law rise and fall together, Congress by Congress. The waves usually follow a change in who is allowed to decide, or in what the national budget will pay for.

When the Barrio Charter of 1959 let provincial boards create and rename barrios, Congress mostly stopped doing either. When a 2017 law made tuition free at state universities and colleges, laws for new campuses came back. Between the 13th and 14th Congresses, the number of laws splitting annexes from their parent high schools went from 2 to 151.

Each panel counts laws per Congress and has its own scale, printed at the top. The break separates the 1st to 7th Congresses (1946 to 1972) from the 8th to 20th (1987 on). The 20th Congress is still in session.

Bills

Filed, then mostly forgotten

Laws are the survivors. BetterGov's open record of the House of Representatives lists every House bill filed since 1987, 143,156 of them. We sorted their titles with the same rules we used for the laws.

The first Congress under the 1987 Constitution stands out. Its members filed 35,168 bills, and 87% asked for something in one place: a school building, a barangay hall, a deep well, a stretch of concrete road. That Congress passed 786 local laws in all, so at most 3 in 100 of these bills became law.

The flood peaked in 1988, with 15,182 local bills in a single year, and had already receded by 1990, when the national budget began giving each member a lump sum for projects in their district: the Countrywide Development Fund, later the PDAF. No year since has come close; the most was 5,285.

Promises

Beds on paper

A law that raises a hospital's bed capacity does not build a ward. The money has to come later, from the budget. So we checked every such law against PhilHealth's list of accredited hospitals, which gives the beds each hospital actually runs.

We matched 52 government hospitals whose bed capacity Congress raised by law since 2016. 46 of them are accredited today for fewer beds than their law says. The newest laws have had little time to take effect, so look at the older ones: of the 43 hospitals whose law passed from 2016 to 2022, 37 are still below it.

A gap is not proof that nothing happened. Hospitals grow in stages, and PhilHealth accredits the beds that are licensed and in use, not the ones a law allows. But the gaps are large and recent, which suggests many of these laws passed without the money to carry them out.

Schools: mostly real, often already there

School laws hold up better. Of the 1,557 laws from 1987 to 2023 that established, separated or converted a public school, we found the school the law names, in the right town, in DepEd's lists for 85%. We could not find 5%.

The telling number is about timing. DepEd's lists begin in 2017, so for laws passed since 2019 we can see whether the school existed before the law. Of the 107 we matched, 68% were in DepEd's list before the law was approved, including 22 of the 35 laws that "establish" a school. Many school laws give a new status or name to a school that is already running.

Authors

Who writes them

For bills filed since 2004, the House record names a principal author. We linked 2,097 local laws to the House bill they came from, and 1,748 of them to a district representative in OpenHalalan's record of winners, which tells us the province they represent.

A common claim is that political families use laws like these to reward their base. We can test a simple version. A seat is a family seat when someone else with the representative's surname won the governorship, the vice governorship, another House seat, or a mayoralty in the same province in the same election, or held a House seat there in one of the two elections before. By this rule 49% of district terms were family seats.

Family seats did not produce more local laws. Representatives in family seats were the principal authors of 1.21 local laws per term. Other representatives averaged 1.37. The intervals overlap, and the gap is not consistent from one Congress to the next: family seats averaged more in one Congress, about the same in two, and fewer in four.

Surnames are a rough marker of kinship. Unrelated people can share a common surname, and relatives can have different ones. OpenHalalan's records for 2004 to 2013 come from an older source that could not be checked against ballots. We report averages over many seats, not claims about any one person.

Calendar

Eighty years of signing days

84% of laws before martial law were approved in June, after each regular session closed in May. Since 1987 the share is 10%, and laws are signed all year.

Data

How this was made

Sources

  • LawPhil Project (Arellano Law Foundation): the list and full text of every Republic Act. 309 acts missing from LawPhil come from the Chan Robles Virtual Law Library, and 22 that neither library has, including eight national budgets, from the House record.
  • BetterGov open-congress-data: every House bill since 1987, its author and its history (CC0).
  • OpenHalalan (Leung, Alejandro, Acuna, Buot, Go and Nable 2026): winners of House, provincial and municipal races (ODbL).
  • DepEd machine-ready files: public schools and enrolment, school years 2017-18 to 2025-26.
  • PhilHealth: accredited hospitals and infirmaries, July 31, 2026.
  • Philippine Internal Migration Dataset: the PSGC list of towns, former names and census populations (PSA).
  • faeldon/philippines-json-maps: PSA 2023 boundaries of provinces and towns.

How the laws were sorted

Titles of Philippine laws follow fixed formulas ("An Act Establishing a National High School in Barangay ..."), so an ordered list of text rules sorts them well. We checked the rules against 400 laws drawn at random and labelled by hand: they agreed on 91.5% of laws for the nineteen kinds and 96.8% for local, private or national. After fixing the rules that failed, a fresh sample of 200 gave 92.0% and 98.5%.

Places come from the titles. A town counts only when its name is unique in the country or it sits inside a province the title also names. The enacting Congress comes from the act's header, else the dates the chambers passed it, else the House record, else the approval date.

What this cannot tell you

  • Titles describe laws, not their effects. A law can be local in title and national in effect, and the reverse.
  • 3 act numbers are in no source we could find.
  • Vetoed bills, and bills that died in the Senate, appear only through the House record.
  • A school or hospital we did not find may exist under another name, or in a list we did not check.
  • Authors and family seats cover the 13th to 19th Congresses and the district representatives we could match to OpenHalalan's list of winners. Party-list members are not included.

Files

  • laws.csv: one row per Republic Act, with its kind, places, sources and links.
  • school_laws.csv and hospital_laws.csv: each law with the school or hospital we matched.
  • law_authors.csv and rep_terms.csv: authors and representative terms.
